In development

We are developing a bedside urine test for NEC. For babies with no blood to spare.

A card for necrotizing enterocolitis, designed to be low-cost and read by a tablet or phone, with no blood draw and no lab.

NEC is found late. By then the bowel is already injured.

Early signs are non-specific, and imaging confirms injury once it is under way. Today the marker can only be measured from a blood draw or by laboratory ELISA, which takes at least 3.5 hours. Our card is designed to give a reading in under five minutes.

~56,000U.S. babies a year are born under 1,500 g
7–10%of them develop NEC
10–30%of babies with NEC do not survive

In one small study, the marker rose before the signs.

I-FABP is released when the cells lining the small bowel are injured, and is small enough to pass into urine.

Published laboratory result · not our card

In 62 very-low-birth-weight infants, 5 of whom developed NEC, a urinary I-FABP:creatinine ratio above 10.2 pg/nmol the day before clinical signs was reported to identify NEC with 100% sensitivity and 95.6% specificity.

Gollin et al., Neonatology 2014;106(3):195–200. Measured by laboratory ELISA.

Honest limits. Small studies, few NEC cases. Nothing has yet been measured on our card. Closing that gap is the development plan.

Five steps at the bedside.

  1. 01

    Collect

    A few drops of urine from a cotton ball in the diaper.

  2. 02

    Apply

    An I-FABP test line plus a creatinine pad to correct for dilution.

  3. 03

    Read

    The nurse photographs the card with a unit tablet or phone.

  4. 04

    Compute

    The app plots the ratio against the baby’s own daily trend.

  5. 05

    Act

    A rising ratio prompts review and imaging. The team decides.

Proposed intended use · working draft
“An aid in assessing the risk of necrotizing enterocolitis in preterm infants in the NICU, used together with clinical findings and imaging.”

An aid, not a verdict. It is not for ruling NEC out, or for replacing imaging or clinical judgement.
